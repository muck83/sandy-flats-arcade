Overview

Running the task

Source. The task follows the build-up of the Mathematics Vision Project's Secondary Mathematics I Honors, Module 1: Systems (the "Pet Sitters" sequence): constraints in words, then inequalities, then graphs, then a feasible region and a decision. The context, wording and numbers here are new and pitched at Grade 8.

Mathematics. Writing and solving one- and two-step inequalities in one variable, graphing them on number lines (open or closed dots), writing linear inequalities in two variables, rewriting standard form as slope-intercept form, the meaning of slope, graphing boundary lines (solid or dashed) with intercepts, shading half-planes with a test point, and reading corners of a feasible region. Systems are solved only by graphing and checking, so the task works before students have learned elimination.

Lesson slides. Each lesson has a slide deck that runs with this table: Inequalities 3 (launch + Part 1), 4 (Part 2), 5 (Part 3), 6 (Part 4) and 7 (Part 5). Before the launch, two lessons build the basics: Inequalities 1 (number lines, solving, turning the sign, a word problem with rounding) and Inequalities 2 (testing points, solid and dashed lines, shading on the plane). Every deck has the same rhythm: about 12 minutes of hands-on slides on the skills that part needs, taught on a parallel problem (the Pond Boat Hire: kayaks and pedal boats, different numbers, so nothing gives the boathouse away), then work time on the part, opened from the slides, then the part's own self-check as a quick question you see on the console.

Collecting the parts. Collect the paper parts at the end of each lesson, or have students keep them in a folder, so nothing is lost before Part 5. Students on a computer can use the same browser every time and print everything to PDF at the end.

Support. Read the situation aloud. Give a partly filled intercept table (question 10) or let students check one inequality with you before they go on. The ✓/✗ table (question 7) can be done in pairs. Stretch. After question 18: "At what daily takings for an air hockey table do (9, 7) and (6, 8) tie?" (Answer: $60, because 3 claw machines × $20 = 1 table × $60.)

Answer key

Part 1 · One machine at a time

1.Prize deal
c ≥ 4. Closed dot at 4, arrow to the right (whole numbers only).
Common error: c > 4 or an open dot ("at least 4" includes 4).
2.Stock
h ≤ 8, and since h can't be negative, 0 ≤ h ≤ 8. Closed dots at 0 and 8. Accept h ≤ 8 with an arrow to the left for Emerging. The full credit answer notices h ≥ 0.
3.Claw machines only (h = 0)
a) 2c ≤ 60, so c ≤ 30.
b) 200c ≤ 4400, so c ≤ 22.
c) 20c > 300, so c > 15. Open dot at 15.
d) 15 < c ≤ 22 (the prize deal c ≥ 4 is already met). Whole numbers: 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, so 7 possible plans.
Common errors: a closed dot at 15. Taking the larger upper limit (30) instead of the one that is met by both (22). Listing 15 as a whole-number answer.
4.Exactly 4 claw machines
a) 2(4) + 6h ≤ 60 → 6h ≤ 52 → h ≤ 8⅔ (8.67), so at most 8 whole tables.
b) 200(4) + 350h ≤ 4400 → 350h ≤ 3600 → h ≤ 10.29 (10²⁄₇).
c) 20(4) + 50h > 300 → 50h > 220 → h > 4.4.
d) With the stock limit h ≤ 8: 4.4 < h ≤ 8. Whole numbers: 5, 6, 7, 8.
Common errors: forgetting to subtract the claw machines' share first (writing 6h ≤ 60). Rounding 8⅔ up to 9.
Answer key

Part 2 · Both machines at once

5.Two-variable inequalities
a) Floor space: 2c + 6h ≤ 60 (2c = m² used by claw machines, 6h = m² used by air hockey).
b) Budget: 200c + 350h ≤ 4400 (equivalent: 4c + 7h ≤ 88).
c) Staff pay: 20c + 50h > 300. Strict, because the takings must be more than $300. Exactly $300 is not enough.
Common errors: swapping coefficients (6c + 2h). Using the daily takings ($20, $50) in the budget inequality. Writing ≥ 300.
6.The system
c ≥ 4 · 0 ≤ h ≤ 8 · 2c + 6h ≤ 60 · 200c + 350h ≤ 4400 · 20c + 50h > 300, with c and h whole numbers.
7.Testing the plans

8.Slope-intercept form
Floor space: 6h = −2c + 60 → h = −⅓c + 10. Budget: 350h = −200c + 4400 → h = −⁴⁄₇c + ⁸⁸⁄₇ (about 12.57).
9.Meaning of the slope
For every air hockey table they add, they have room for 3 fewer claw machines, because one table uses 6 m², the same floor as three 2 m² claw machines. (Slope −⅓: h goes up 1 when c goes down 3.) For Deep: the budget slope −⁴⁄₇ means 4 tables cost the same as 7 claw machines ($1400).

11.The graph
Test point (0, 0): it passes floor space and budget, so shade below those lines. It fails staff pay (0 > 300 is false), so shade above the dashed line. Keep the side of c = 4 to the right and the side of h = 8 below. The feasible region has six corners: (22, 0), (6, 8) and (4, 8) are whole-number plans; (10.8, 6.4), where floor space meets budget, is not whole numbers; (15, 0) and (4, 4.4) are on the dashed line, so not included.
Common errors: reading the across-scale as 1 per square (it's 2). Shading the whole space below the staff-pay line. A solid staff-pay line. Leaving out c = 4 and h = 8.
12.Where floor space and budget cross (not a whole-number point)
No system solving is needed. On the zoomed grid the lines cross at about (10.8, 6.4); estimates between (10.5, 6.3) and (11, 6.5) are fine. Check (10.8, 6.4): 2(10.8) + 6(6.4) = 21.6 + 38.4 = 60 ✓ and 200(10.8) + 350(6.4) = 2160 + 2240 = 4400 ✓. The arcade can't use it: you can't rent 10.8 claw machines or 6.4 tables. (It would take in $536, the most possible if parts of machines were allowed.)
13.The three plans on the graph
Priya (22, 0) and Gus (6, 8) are on the edge of the region. Edges on solid lines count, so both plans are possible. Marcus (10, 10) is outside: above the floor space and budget lines and above h = 8.

Common errors: giving (10.8, 6.4) or $536 as the answer. Rounding to (11, 6) or (10, 6) without checking others. Rounding up to (11, 7) or (10, 7), which breaks the limits.
17.Reply to Marcus
Ten of each would take in $700, but the arcade can't have it: it needs 80 m² (the boathouse has 60), costs $5500 (they have $4400) and needs 10 air hockey tables (the town has 8). On the graph, (10, 10) is outside the feasible region. The best plan that is possible takes in $530.
18.What if air hockey takes in $70?
M = 20c + 70h: (22, 0) → $440, (9, 7) → $670, (6, 8) → $680, (4, 8) → $640. Yes, the best plan changes to Gus's plan: 6 claw machines and 8 air hockey tables.
Why (Deep): moving from (9, 7) to (6, 8) gives up 3 claw machines and gains 1 air hockey table. At $50 that is −$60 + $50 = −$10, a loss. At $70 it is −$60 + $70 = +$10, a gain.
Common error: saying "air hockey earns more, so rent all 8" without checking the corners. That is right at $70 but was wrong at $50, so the reasoning has to show why.

Part 5 · The finished plan

Mark the finished plan as the main evidence, and look back at the parts only to settle a level the plan leaves unclear (for example, a graph redrawn badly in Part 5 that was right in Part 3). A strong plan has:

  • The question and the two variables at the top.
  • A limits table with five rows: words, inequality, and number lines for c ≥ 4 and 0 ≤ h ≤ 8.
  • The system, written together.
  • A clean graph like the one in question 11, with the friends' plans marked.
  • The corner table, with (10.8, 6.4) marked as not whole numbers, and the whole-number hunt showing (9, 7) → $530.
  • A recommendation: "Rent 9 claw machines and 7 air hockey tables for $530 a day. The best corner, 10.8 and 6.4, can't be rented, and rounding it down only makes $500. Marcus's plan isn't possible (floor, budget and stock). Gus's plan takes in $520 and Priya's $440. If air hockey takes in $70 a day, switch to Gus's plan: $680."

Hand-in. Students upload a photo, PDF or pitch video to the Unit 2 Summative assignment on Toddle (this is what the student handout says). Mark each upload on the task sheet and give the PDF back on Toddle. Check each upload is readable on the day, while missing pages can still be photographed.

Teacher notes

Making it come alive, and scaffolds

For everyone

  • Boathouse Planner launch (lesson 1, 10 minutes): pairs try to beat the three friends. Collect the best plans on the board, but don't confirm a winner. That's what Parts 1 to 4 are for. With ?region on the address, the planner shades the feasible region for the Part 3 discussion.
  • Paper floor plan (support pack page 6): good for students who need to touch it. It also raises a fair question: tables don't always fit even when the area adds up. The task models area only, and that is a good thing for students to say in the plan.
  • News flash cards make question 19 personal, and copying harder.
  • Choice of format in Part 5: plan, poster or 90-second pitch video. Every format needs the same six parts, so the rubric doesn't change.
  • Translation help: a 🌐 picker at the top of the handout and the support pack shows Arabic, Korean, French or Spanish under the English (situation, questions, goals, self-checks, Part 5, hints, news flash cards) plus a key-word list. It prints too, so you can print bilingual paper copies. Students still write in the language you choose.
  • Zoomed grid (question 12): students plot two easy points on each line and estimate the crossing. No system solving is needed, and the check of (10.8, 6.4) is only substitution.
  • Self-checks at the end of each part give a check (counts, sums) without the answers.

For students who struggle

  • ★ Core questions (1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15, 16) are the route to start with. They give evidence up to Emerging and most of Developed. Full Developed also needs 4, 8, 9, 12 and 17. Deep needs 18 and 19.
  • 💡 Hints: three levels per question (a nudge, the first step, an example from the Taco Truck). On the computer, the handout prints "Hints used: n of 3" under each question. On paper, hand out the hint cards one at a time.
  • Support pack: symbol card, the Taco Truck worked example (same steps, different numbers), half-filled tables for questions 7, 10 and 15, a starter graph with two lines drawn, and sentence frames.
  • Marking with support: mark the level the work shows. Note the support used on the task sheet. A student who needed the Example hint on most graphing questions is probably not yet secure at Developed in Graphing, even if the graph is right.
